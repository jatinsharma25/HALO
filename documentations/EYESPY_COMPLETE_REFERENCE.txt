1. ELEVATOR PITCHES
================================================================================

10-SECOND VERSION:
"EyeSpy turns existing CCTV into an intelligent security layer that detects
real safety and security events in real time, explains every alert in plain
language, and produces cryptographically tamper-proof evidence — without ever
identifying who anyone is."

30-SECOND VERSION:
"Most 'AI CCTV' products either do nothing smart at all, or they do
facial recognition and biometric tracking, which is a privacy and legal
minefield. EyeSpy sits in between: it detects behavior — restricted-zone
entry, loitering, falls, abandoned objects, fire/smoke, crowding, camera
tampering — using person detection only, never identity. Every alert comes
with a deterministic, human-readable explanation of exactly which rule fired
and why, not a black-box confidence number. And every piece of evidence it
produces is cryptographically signed and logged in a tamper-evident audit
trail, so it can actually be trusted and used, not just generated."

2-MINUTE VERSION:
"Security camera footage today is either watched passively by a human who
loses attention after 20 minutes, or it's run through 'AI analytics' that
either over-promise with facial recognition and identity tracking, or
under-deliver with an opaque AI score nobody can explain or trust as
evidence.

EyeSpy is built around three ideas. First: detect behavior, not identity.
We run YOLOv8n restricted to the 'person' class only — no face recognition,
no biometrics, no cross-camera person re-identification, ever. Second:
every judgment call is deterministic and explainable. Zone rules, loitering
thresholds, fall/abandoned-object/fire-smoke heuristics, crowd thresholds —
each one is a fixed, auditable rule, not a model's opinion, and each alert
reports both an impact score and a confidence score separately, so an
unreliable heuristic can never masquerade as a certain detection. Third:
evidence has to survive scrutiny. Every exported clip is dual-signed — HMAC
for internal proof, Ed25519 for third-party verification — with whole-person
redaction by default, and every operator action is written into a
hash-chained audit log that makes tampering after the fact detectable.

It runs on FastAPI, YOLOv8n, and a React dashboard, works with the CCTV
cameras a building already has, requires no cloud dependency, and never
takes an autonomous action — every alert goes to a human. It's built to be
the kind of system a campus, hospital, or government facility could actually
deploy and defend, not just demo."


================================================================================
2. THE PROBLEM THIS EXISTS TO SOLVE
================================================================================

- CCTV coverage has grown enormously, but the monitoring model hasn't. Most
  installations rely on a human operator watching multiple live feeds.
  Sustained-attention research on exactly this task shows meaningful
  degradation in detection accuracy after about 20 minutes of continuous
  monitoring. In practice, most incidents on passively-monitored CCTV are
  discovered after the fact, as evidence, not caught in real time.

- Where "AI surveillance" has been added, it tends to go to one of two
  unsatisfying extremes:
    (a) Vendors sell facial recognition / biometric identification as the
        headline feature. This creates real privacy, consent, and legal
        exposure, and is increasingly restricted or banned outright in many
        jurisdictions (India's DPDP Act, EU GDPR/AI Act, and multiple
        municipal facial-recognition bans in the US are all pointed the
        same direction).
    (b) Or the "AI" is a black-box confidence score with no explanation,
        which means nobody can tell an operator WHY an alert fired, and the
        output is useless as defensible evidence in an actual incident
        review, HR case, or legal proceeding, because nobody can explain
        how the system arrived at its conclusion.

- There is a real, underserved middle ground: systems that are genuinely
  useful for real-time situational awareness and produce evidence that can
  be trusted and explained — without identifying anyone, without deciding
  anything autonomously, and without being a black box. That's the gap
  EyeSpy targets.


================================================================================
3. WHAT EYESPY IS — AND EXPLICITLY IS NOT
================================================================================

IS:
- A real-time detection layer on top of existing camera infrastructure
  (webcam, USB CCTV, RTSP IP cameras, or recorded video files).
- Person-presence and behavior detection: is someone in a zone they
  shouldn't be, have they been stationary too long, has someone fallen,
  is an object sitting unattended, is there a fire/smoke-colored region,
  did someone cross a monitored line, is a crowd forming.
- Camera self-health monitoring: is a camera actually working, or has it
  gone offline, frozen, been blacked out/covered, or gone severely blurry.
- A deterministic scoring and explanation engine — every incident's impact
  and confidence score, and its plain-English explanation, is computed by
  fixed code, not a model, and is 100% reproducible from the same inputs.
- A tamper-evident evidence and audit system — dual-signed clips, redacted
  by default, and a hash-chained log of every operator action.
- Strictly human-in-the-loop: every alert is surfaced to an operator for
  review; nothing in the system takes automatic real-world action.

IS NOT (permanently, by design — not "not yet"):
- Facial recognition or any biometric identification.
- Cross-camera person re-identification / identity tracking. Track IDs are
  anonymous and camera-local only; they mean nothing outside that one
  camera's current session.
- An autonomous response system (no auto-locking doors, no auto-dispatch to
  authorities, no automatic escalation without a human in the loop).
- A generative-AI or LLM-based decision system anywhere in the detection,
  scoring, or explanation path. Every explanation is a template filled in
  from concrete rule inputs — there is no language model deciding anything.

4. FULL SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE
================================================================================

HIGH-LEVEL DATA FLOW
---------------------

  Camera (webcam index / RTSP URL / recorded .mp4)
        |
        v
  VideoSource  (one dedicated Python thread per camera, ~30fps read loop)
        |  - decodes frames, resizes to 1280x720
        |  - feeds each frame through CameraHealthMonitor
        |  - auto-reconnects the capture handle on sustained read failure
        v
  DetectionPipeline  (one dedicated thread per camera)
        |  - YOLOv8n inference, filtered to person class (COCO id 0) only
        |  - CentroidTracker assigns/holds anonymous per-camera track IDs
        |  - per-person: zone-hit test, after-hours test, loitering timer
        |  - whole-frame: fall heuristic, abandoned-object heuristic,
        |    fire/smoke heuristic, line-crossing counter, crowd heuristic
        |  - draws the annotated overlay frame for the live view
        |  - writes an Observation row to the DB for every fired event,
        |    with confidence_score and impact_score computed and stored
        |    SEPARATELY (this separation is a deliberate design principle,
        |    not an implementation detail — see Section 6)
        v
  Correlator  (backend/incidents/correlator.py)
        |  - dedups by (camera_id, event_type, 5-minute time bucket) so
        |    repeated firings of the same rule in the same window become
        |    ONE incident, not a flood of separate incidents
        |  - if impact_score >= 0.6, creates or extends an Incident
        |  - calls the deterministic scoring engine (scoring.py) and
        |    explanation engine (explain.py) to (re)compute the incident's
        |    impact_score, confidence_score, and human-readable explanation
        |    from ALL of its linked observations
        v
  Evidence packaging  (on-demand, via API call, not automatic)
        |  - redacts every detected person in the clip (whole bounding box,
        |    not just the face — see Section 6 for why)
        |  - computes SHA-256 of the clip, then:
        |      * HMAC-SHA256 of that hash, using a service secret
        |        (proves "this came from us" to anyone holding the secret)
        |      * Ed25519 signature of that hash, using a private key whose
        |        public half is published at GET /evidence/public-key
        |        (proves authenticity to a THIRD PARTY who never had
        |        access to our service secret — e.g. a court, an auditor)
        |  - stores both a redacted "public" clip and a role-gated
        |    unredacted "original" clip
        v
  Audit log  (backend/audit/chain.py)
        |  - every sensitive action (incident acknowledged, evidence
        |    viewed/exported, zone changed, etc.) is written as a row whose
        |    hash includes the PREVIOUS row's hash (a hash chain)
        |  - if any row is edited after the fact, every subsequent hash
        |    stops matching — tampering becomes mathematically detectable,
        |    not just "we hope nobody edited the database"
        v
  FastAPI REST API  (JWT auth, role-checked on every route)
        v
  React dashboard
        - live camera tiles (MJPEG stream, per-tile Start/Stop for
          admin/operator, view-only for responder)
        - incident triage queue with explanation, evidence, and
          acknowledge/escalate/resolve workflow
        - governance view: audit log with hash-chain verification, evidence
          signing public key, SOPs
        - configuration: cameras, zones (polygon editor), schedules
        - live push updates over WebSocket (new alert / alert acknowledged)
          PLUS an 8-second REST poll as a safety net — see Section 5 for why
          both exist instead of just one


WHY TWO THREADS PER CAMERA (VideoSource + DetectionPipeline), NOT ONE?
------------------------------------------------------------------------
Reading frames and running detection are different pacing problems: the
camera keeps delivering frames at its own native rate regardless of how
long detection takes on the previous one. Splitting them means a slow
detection pass (heavier when more people are in frame) never blocks frame
capture, and the live "raw" feed can still update even if the detection
pipeline briefly falls behind. If they were one thread, a slow detection
frame would directly stall frame capture, and a stalled capture couldn't be
told apart from an actual dead camera.


================================================================================
5. COMPLETE TECH STACK — AND WHY EACH PIECE WAS CHOSEN
================================================================================

BACKEND FRAMEWORK: FastAPI (not Django / Flask)
------------------------------------------------
- Native async support matters here: the MJPEG stream endpoint and the
  WebSocket alert feed are both long-lived connections that need to coexist
  with normal short request/response API calls without blocking a worker.
- Automatic request/response validation and OpenAPI docs (GET /docs) via
  Pydantic models, with no extra library needed — useful both for
  development speed and for anyone integrating against the API later.
- Flask would need bolt-on async and API-schema tooling to match this.
  Django is heavier than needed here — this isn't a content-management or
  admin-heavy app, it's a fairly small, focused API surface, and Django's
  batteries (ORM conventions, admin site, templating) aren't the ones this
  project needed; SQLAlchemy + Alembic already cover the data layer more
  directly for this use case.

DATABASE: SQLite (with SQLAlchemy so Postgres is a connection-string away)
------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Zero setup: one file, no separate DB server to install/run/secure for a
  demo, a single-site deployment, or a small campus. This matters a lot for
  something meant to be easy to pilot.
- Still fully ACID — this is not "a toy database," it's a real relational
  engine with real transactions and real constraints (CHECK constraints
  enforce valid event types, status values, score ranges, etc. at the DB
  level, not just in application code).
- Because the whole data layer goes through SQLAlchemy's ORM rather than
  raw SQL, moving to Postgres for a larger, multi-node deployment is
  changing DATABASE_URL, not rewriting queries. That migration path exists
  by construction, even though it hasn't been load-tested yet (see
  Limitations).
- Why not go straight to Postgres from day one? Because for the actual
  target of "one building, one small server, a handful to a few dozen
  cameras," a separate DB server is operational overhead with no benefit —
  SQLite's single-writer model is not a bottleneck at that scale.

MIGRATIONS: Alembic (not manual schema management or "just recreate the DB")
------------------------------------------------------------------------
- SQLite can't ALTER a CHECK constraint in place — the only way to change
  one is to rebuild the table. Alembic's "batch mode"
  (`op.batch_alter_table(..., recreate="always")`) does exactly that
  rebuild safely, preserving existing data, and this project genuinely
  needed it multiple times (adding new event types as new detection
  features were built — see migrations 005, 007, 008). Without a real
  migration tool, every schema change would risk silently losing data or
  require a manual, error-prone table-rebuild script each time.

DETECTION MODEL: YOLOv8n (not YOLOv5/v8s-m-l, not Faster R-CNN/SSD, not a
custom-trained model)
------------------------------------------------------------------------
- "n" (nano) variant specifically: this system is CPU-inference-first (no
  GPU assumed), and nano is the size built for that — it trades some
  accuracy for roughly 3-5x the speed of the "s"/"m" variants on CPU, which
  is the difference between a usable frame rate and an unusable one on
  commodity hardware. If GPU deployment becomes standard (see Future
  Roadmap), a larger variant becomes viable and would be a config change,
  not an architecture change.
- YOLO family over two-stage detectors (Faster R-CNN) or SSD: YOLO's
  single-pass design is simply faster for the same rough accuracy class,
  and speed is the binding constraint here, not squeezing out the last
  percent of mAP.
- A pretrained COCO model, not a custom-trained one: person detection is
  one of the best-covered, most heavily benchmarked classes in COCO —
  there's no domain-specific visual category here (like a specific
  industrial defect) that would justify the cost and risk of training a
  custom model from scratch. We only ever use ONE of its 80 classes
  (person, class ID 0) and discard every other prediction.

TRACKING: A custom centroid tracker (not DeepSORT / ByteTrack / StrongSORT)
------------------------------------------------------------------------
- The tracker only needs to answer "is this the same person as one frame
  ago, for THIS camera, right now" — it does not need to survive long
  occlusions, re-identify someone who left and came back, or produce an
  identity that means anything outside the current session. That's a
  deliberately narrow job.
- DeepSORT/ByteTrack-class trackers add a Kalman filter, a Hungarian-
  algorithm assignment step, and (for DeepSORT) an appearance-embedding
  network purely to track more robustly through occlusion and re-identify
  a track after it's lost — capability this system explicitly does NOT
  want to have, since a track surviving loss-and-reacquisition starts to
  edge toward "identity," which conflicts with the anonymous, camera-local-
  only tracking principle. A simple nearest-centroid match
  (max 120px jump, drop after 50 missed frames) does the actual job needed
  — "count/dedupe people currently visible" — with far less code and
  compute, and with a track ID that's cheap to prove is meaningless outside
  the moment it's assigned.

CLASSICAL CV HEURISTICS FOR FALL / ABANDONED OBJECT / FIRE-SMOKE
(not trained classifiers for each)
------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Training a reliable "fall detector" or "fire detector" model needs a
  large, well-labeled, situation-specific video dataset (falls, real fires,
  from CCTV-realistic angles) that doesn't exist off-the-shelf in the
  quantity needed for a trustworthy model, and collecting/labeling one is a
  project on its own.
- Classical CV (bounding-box aspect-ratio change for falls, background-
  subtraction + non-overlap-with-person for abandoned objects, HSV color-
  range + sustained-area for fire/smoke) is fully deterministic, requires
  zero training data, and — critically — its FAILURE MODES are understandable
  and explainable ("the box went wide and flat" is something a human can
  sanity-check; a neural net's internal activation is not). Given that
  every one of these is explicitly a "flag for human review" feature, not a
  "trust this automatically" feature, deterministic and explainable beats
  marginally-more-accurate-but-opaque for this specific use case.
- The tradeoff is honestly worse raw accuracy than a well-trained model
  would eventually achieve — which is exactly why their confidence scores
  are hard-capped low in scoring.py (see Section 6) rather than presented
  as equally trustworthy to the person-detection signal itself.

FRONTEND FRAMEWORK: React 19 + Vite (not Vue/Angular/Svelte, not Create
React App/webpack)
------------------------------------------------------------------------
- React: largest ecosystem for the specific pieces this UI needed
  (headless component primitives, data-fetching libraries, table/dialog/
  tooltip patterns) — for a UI-heavy dashboard (camera grid, incident
  triage, zone polygon editor, audit log viewer), ecosystem breadth mattered
  more than any framework-level performance difference.
- Vite over webpack/CRA: near-instant dev-server start and hot-module-
  reload, which mattered a lot given how much of this project's iteration
  was "make a change, immediately look at the live camera feed/dashboard."
  Create React App is also effectively unmaintained at this point.

STYLING: Tailwind CSS + shadcn/ui (not Bootstrap, not Material UI/Ant Design,
not hand-rolled CSS-only)
------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Tailwind: utility classes plus CSS custom-property design tokens made it
  straightforward to build a real light/dark theme system (see
  frontend/src/index.css) without maintaining two parallel stylesheets —
  the same utility classes just resolve against whichever token set is
  active.
- shadcn/ui over Material UI/Ant Design/Chakra: shadcn isn't a component
  library you install as a black-box dependency — it generates the actual
  component source into the project (frontend/src/components/ui/), built on
  unstyled Radix primitives for accessibility (focus trapping, ARIA
  attributes, keyboard nav) already solved correctly. That means full
  control to restyle/modify every component (which we did — extending
  Badge variants for camera-health states, for example) without fighting a
  library's theming API or being blocked on an upstream release for a
  small tweak.

DATA FETCHING: React Query (not plain fetch/useEffect, not Redux)
------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Handles caching, request de-duplication, background refetching (the
  8-second polling), and cache invalidation (triggered by WebSocket events)
  with a fraction of the boilerplate a hand-rolled fetch/useEffect/useState
  pattern needs, and without needing a global state library (Redux) for
  what is fundamentally server-state, not client-state.

LIVE UPDATES: WebSocket AND 8-second polling, not just one or the other
------------------------------------------------------------------------
- WebSocket alone: fast, but any single dropped/missed message (a brief
  network blip, a reconnect race) means the UI silently goes stale with no
  self-correction — nothing else would ever refresh it.
- Polling alone: correct eventually, but a genuinely new incident could sit
  unseen for up to 8 seconds, which matters for a security alert.
- Together: WebSocket gives near-instant push the moment something
  happens (new alert, alert acknowledged elsewhere), and the poll is a
  cheap safety net that guarantees the UI is never more than 8 seconds
  stale even if a push is missed. Belt and suspenders, for a genuinely
  low cost.

VIDEO STREAMING: MJPEG (not WebRTC, not HLS/DASH)
------------------------------------------------------------------------
- MJPEG is just a long-lived HTTP response of concatenated JPEG frames — an
  `<img src="/stream/id?token=...">` tag is the entire client-side
  implementation, with browser-native reconnection behavior on drop.
- WebRTC would add real complexity (signaling, ICE/STUN, SDP negotiation)
  to solve a problem this project doesn't have — sub-second glass-to-glass
  latency for two-way video calling. This is one-way, dashboard-side
  monitoring where ~100-300ms of extra latency is a complete non-issue.
- HLS/DASH are built for adaptive-bitrate, CDN-distributed video-on-demand
  or broadcast — solving problems (multi-resolution renditions, edge
  caching, DVR-style seeking) this single-viewer, single-source, low-
  camera-count internal tool does not have, at the cost of several seconds
  of segment-based latency that would actually hurt here.

AUTH: JWT (not server-side sessions)
------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Stateless: the API doesn't need a shared session store, which keeps
  horizontal scaling simple later (any backend instance can validate a
  token without a shared session backend) and keeps the auth layer simple
  for a system that may need to run fully air-gapped/on-prem with no
  external session infrastructure.
- Role is embedded directly in the token payload and checked on every
  route via a `require_role(...)` dependency — RBAC enforcement lives at
  the API layer, not just hidden in what the UI chooses to show.

EVIDENCE SIGNING: HMAC-SHA256 + Ed25519 together (not just one, not a
blockchain)
------------------------------------------------------------------------
- These prove two DIFFERENT things to two DIFFERENT audiences:
    * HMAC-SHA256 (symmetric, shared service secret) proves "this evidence
      came from our system" to anyone who holds that secret — i.e., us,
      internally.
    * Ed25519 (asymmetric) proves the same thing to a THIRD PARTY who only
      has the PUBLIC key (published at GET /evidence/public-key) and never
      had access to the private signing key or the HMAC secret — e.g. a
      court, an external auditor, another organization. This is the
      signature that actually matters once a clip leaves the system's own
      custody.
- Why not "just" one of them: HMAC alone can't be verified by an outside
  party without handing them the same secret used to sign everything else
  (which then lets them forge new "signed" evidence too) — it only proves
  internal authenticity. Ed25519 alone is more expensive to verify at
  scale for purely-internal checks where a shared secret is fine and
  faster.
- Why not a blockchain: nothing about this problem needs decentralized
  consensus, multiple untrusted parties agreeing on order, or a
  distributed ledger. A single organization's evidence chain of custody
  is served entirely by a straightforward hash chain (see the audit log
  design below) — a blockchain would add substantial operational
  complexity and cost for zero corresponding benefit here.

AUDIT LOG: A hash chain (not a blockchain, not "just" timestamped rows)
------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Every audit row stores sha256(previous_row_hash + timestamp + action +
  actor_id + payload). Change or delete any past row and every row after
  it fails to re-hash correctly — tampering is detectable by simply
  re-walking the chain (GET /audit/verify), without needing a second
  system, a blockchain, or write-once storage hardware.
- Plain timestamped rows with no chaining would let anyone with DB access
  quietly edit or delete a row with no trace at all — the chain is what
  turns "we have a log" into "we can prove the log wasn't altered."

CONTAINERIZATION: Docker + docker-compose (with a documented non-Docker path)
------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Consistent environment for anyone evaluating/deploying it, without
  requiring them to have the exact right Python/Node versions installed
  natively. But a full non-Docker setup path is also documented (see
  README/CONTRIBUTING), since a from-scratch inspection of the code —
  which matters in a competition/judging context — is a lot easier without
  a container layer between the reader and the running system.

TESTING: pytest (backend) — 133 tests
------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Standard, minimal-boilerplate choice for a Python backend; pytest's
  fixture system made it straightforward to spin up an isolated in-memory
  DB per test and a fresh FastAPI TestClient without hand-rolled setup
  code repeated in every file.


================================================================================
6. EVERY DETECTION FEATURE, IN FULL DETAIL
================================================================================

For every heuristic below: it draws an overlay on the annotated frame on
EVERY evaluation (so an operator watching live always sees what the system
is "thinking"), but only WRITES an Observation to the database when it
actually fires — and firing is itself cooldown-gated, so a sustained
condition doesn't flood the database with duplicate rows every single
frame.

PERSON DETECTION (foundation for everything else)
- YOLOv8n, filtered to COCO class 0 (person) only, confidence threshold 0.5.
  Every other one of YOLO's 80 trainable classes is detected internally by
  the model but explicitly discarded before anything downstream ever sees
  it.

ZONE RULES
- Restricted-zone entry: a person's centroid falls inside a zone polygon
  marked "restricted" -> immediate high-impact event (impact 0.9 of 1.0).
- After-hours presence: any tracked person present outside a camera's
  configured operating schedule -> impact 0.7.
- Loitering: a person classified as "stationary" (little centroid movement)
  for longer than a threshold — global default 120 seconds
  (LOITERING_SECONDS), overridable per-zone via Zone.loitering_threshold_s
  -> impact 0.5.

FALL / COLLAPSE HEURISTIC
- Classical geometric heuristic: watches a tracked person's bounding-box
  aspect ratio over time; a sudden shift from a mostly-upright box to a
  wide/flat box, sustained, reads as a possible fall.
- Impact 0.65 (a possible medical emergency justifies a high impact score
  even though the heuristic itself is uncertain) but confidence is HARD-
  CAPPED at 55/100 regardless of anything else — this is the "impact and
  confidence are separate" design principle doing real work: high-impact,
  low-confidence, by design, so it always reads as "worth an operator's
  immediate attention, NOT a confirmed medical event."
- Real limitation, tested directly: needs a mostly front-on, full-body
  camera view. A close/steep webcam angle that only frames head-and-
  shoulders will never produce the box-flattening signal this heuristic
  looks for — this isn't a threshold that needs tuning, it's a genuine
  requirement of the geometric approach.

ABANDONED-OBJECT HEURISTIC
- Background-subtraction based: learns a background model of the static
  scene (needs roughly 300 frames to converge), then flags a static
  foreground blob that (a) isn't overlapping any currently-tracked person
  and (b) has persisted past a dwell threshold.
- Impact 0.35, confidence capped at 50/100.
- Real limitation, tested directly: if a person stays near the object (the
  overlap check fails), or the background model hasn't converged yet,
  it won't fire — this is a real constraint of the technique, not a bug.

FIRE/SMOKE HEURISTIC
- HSV color-range analysis for flame-like color + sustained area over
  time. Explicitly documented in code as "the least reliable heuristic in
  the system."
- Impact 0.75 (life-safety impact if real) but confidence capped at only
  30/100 — the lowest cap of any heuristic, reflecting that color-based
  fire detection has a genuinely high false-positive rate (any sustained
  orange/red region can trigger it) and is never to be treated as
  confirmed without a human looking at the actual footage.

LINE-CROSSING
- A configurable virtual line; tracks entry/exit direction as tracked
  people cross it. Impact deliberately low (0.2) since a monitored line
  sees routine foot traffic constantly and there's no "wrong direction"
  policy layer yet (see Future Roadmap) — logged for analytics/audit,
  not treated as inherently alarming.
- Confidence 0.85 — this one is NOT capped low like the three above,
  because it isn't inferring a fuzzy visual state; it's a straightforward
  geometric crossing test on an already-reliable tracked position.

CROWD THRESHOLD
- A pure headcount off the same reliably-tracked person boxes: if the
  number of currently-tracked people meets or exceeds a threshold
  (default 5) and stays there for a sustained period (default 10 seconds),
  it fires. Impact 0.45, confidence 0.9 — also NOT capped low, for the
  same reason as line-crossing: counting things already reliably detected
  isn't a fuzzy inference.
- Deliberately global/whole-frame, not per-zone — there's no "expected
  occupancy per zone" config concept yet (Future Roadmap).

CAMERA HEALTH MONITORING
- Offline: no frames received within OFFLINE_TIMEOUT_SECONDS (8s).
- Frozen: frame-to-frame difference below FROZEN_DIFF_THRESHOLD (0.8) for
  the entire FROZEN_WINDOW_SECONDS window (30s — deliberately long, since
  a real, healthy CCTV view of an empty corridor is genuinely near-
  motionless for long stretches; a short window would false-positive on
  every quiet camera).
- Blackout: mean brightness below BLACKOUT_BRIGHTNESS_THRESHOLD (12.0) AND
  standard deviation below BLACKOUT_STD_THRESHOLD (6.0) — both brightness
  AND textureless-ness, so a genuinely dark BUT detailed nighttime IR scene
  doesn't get misread as "covered."
- Blurred: Laplacian variance (edge sharpness) below BLUR_LAPLACIAN_
  THRESHOLD, sustained across every sample in a 3-second window (a single
  noisy frame — autofocus hunting, a blink of motion blur — must not flip
  the status; a genuinely covered/defocused lens will still be caught
  within that short window).
    - This threshold (8.0) is intentionally NOT a universal constant — it's
      camera- and lighting-dependent, overridable per deployment via env
      var. It was recalibrated during development after discovering a
      real consumer webcam sits around 13-14 raw variance even in sharp
      focus (see Section 11) — a value tuned on one camera without
      verifying against real hardware would have been wrong everywhere
      else.
- Auto-reconnect: on sustained read failure (not just a dark frame — an
  actual failure to read from the capture device, which some camera
  drivers exhibit under sustained near-total darkness), the system
  releases and reopens the capture handle on a 5-second backoff, instead
  of retrying forever on a broken handle that will never recover on its
  own.
- Design choice: OFFLINE/FROZEN/BLACKOUT are "hard anomalies" — normal
  detection processing is skipped entirely, since there's no usable frame
  content to detect anything in. BLURRED is treated differently — it's a
  quality WARNING, not a "nothing to see" state, so person detection keeps
  running through it (a blurry frame can still contain a detectable
  person). This distinction was the direct fix for a real bug — see
  Section 11.


================================================================================
7. DATA MODEL & LIFECYCLE
================================================================================

Observation (raw event row)
  - camera_id, track_id (anonymous, camera-local), event_type, timestamp,
    zone_id (nullable), confidence_score, impact_score, explanation,
    raw_metadata (JSON: bbox/dwell time/etc — NEVER face data, by
    construction), acknowledged/acknowledged_by/acknowledged_at.
  - event_type is DB-CHECK-CONSTRAINED to a fixed enum (restricted_zone_
    entry, after_hours_presence, loitering, camera_offline, camera_frozen,
    camera_blackout, camera_blur, fall_warning, abandoned_object_warning,
    fire_smoke_warning, line_crossing, crowd_threshold) — an invalid event
    type can't even be written to the database, not just rejected by
    application code.

Incident (correlated group of observations)
  - Created/extended by the Correlator when an Observation's impact_score
    crosses 0.6. Deduped by (camera_id, event_type, 5-minute time bucket)
    scoped to OPEN incidents only (new/acknowledged/investigating) — a
    resolved incident's dedup hash can legitimately recur once a new
    occurrence of the same rule fires later; that's correct behavior, not
    a duplicate.
  - impact_score / confidence_score / explanation are recomputed from ALL
    currently-linked observations every time a new one is linked —
    genuinely deterministic: the same set of linked observations always
    produces the same three outputs, byte for byte.
  - Lifecycle: new -> acknowledged -> investigating -> escalated ->
    resolved, plus a human-set disposition (true_positive / false_positive
    / uncertain) recorded only on resolution, never inferred automatically.

EvidencePackage
  - Linked to an Incident. clip_path (redacted, public), original_clip_
    path (unredacted, role-gated), manifest_hash (SHA-256 of the clip),
    signature (HMAC), ed25519_signature.

AuditLog
  - entry_hash, prev_hash (genesis row uses "0"), actor_id, action,
    target_type/target_id, timestamp, payload (JSON of what changed).


================================================================================
8. SECURITY, PRIVACY & EVIDENCE-INTEGRITY DESIGN
================================================================================

- No face data is ever stored, in any table, at any point. Redaction uses
  the ALREADY-COMPUTED YOLO person bounding boxes (with a margin), not a
  separate face-detection pass — this was a direct design change after
  early testing showed face-only detectors (Haar cascades) fail routinely
  at CCTV-realistic oblique angles, distance, and resolution. Blurring the
  whole detected person is both more reliable AND strictly more private
  than a face-only approach that would sometimes miss the face and leave
  someone identifiable anyway.
- Track IDs are anonymous integers, assigned per-camera, per-session —
  they carry no identity information and are never linked across cameras.
- Every API route is behind JWT auth and an explicit `require_role(...)`
  check — RBAC is enforced server-side on every request, not just hidden
  in the UI (a responder hitting a start/stop endpoint directly, bypassing
  the UI entirely, still gets a 403).
- Redaction is on by default for the live stream and for exported evidence;
  viewing the unredacted original requires an elevated role and is itself
  written into the audit log.
- The audit log's hash chain means the system's own history of who-did-
  what is independently verifiable (GET /audit/verify walks the whole
  chain and reports the first broken link, if any) — this is what makes
  "we have logs" into "we can prove nobody tampered with the logs."


================================================================================
9. ROLES & ACCESS CONTROL
================================================================================

- admin: full control — camera/zone/schedule configuration, user-facing
  administration, everything operator can do.
- operator: start/stop cameras, acknowledge/escalate/resolve incidents,
  package and view evidence (including unredacted originals).
- responder: read-only camera/incident visibility (can see live feeds and
  incidents, cannot start/stop cameras or change configuration) — added
  specifically so a first-responder role can be looped in for situational
  awareness without giving them control or destructive-action capability.

Every one of these is enforced at the API layer via a `require_role(...)`
FastAPI dependency on each route, not just conditional rendering in the
frontend.

================================================================================
11. REAL BUGS FOUND AND FIXED DURING DEVELOPMENT
================================================================================

This section exists because "we tested it against a live camera and found
real problems" is much stronger material for a judge panel than a system
that has only ever run once in a demo. Every item below actually happened
during this project's development, was diagnosed from first principles
(logs, live testing, targeted instrumentation), and was fixed — not
papered over.

1. BLUR STATE WAS SILENTLY DISABLING ALL DETECTION
   Early on, any camera-health anomaly (offline/frozen/blackout/BLUR) was
   treated identically: skip all detection processing. In practice this
   meant a slightly blurry (but still perfectly usable) webcam feed caused
   the system to stop detecting people entirely — reported by a user as
   "it's not detecting my face" when the real issue was the system
   refusing to even LOOK because of a blur flag. Fix: split camera-health
   states into "hard anomalies" (offline/frozen/blackout — genuinely no
   usable frame) which skip processing, versus "blur" — a quality warning
   that keeps detection running, since a blurry frame can still contain a
   detectable person.

2. BLUR THRESHOLD WAS MISCALIBRATED AGAINST REAL HARDWARE
   The default blur (Laplacian variance) threshold was set assuming
   "sharp" frames read in the hundreds. Live testing on a real consumer
   webcam showed the RAW frame consistently reading around 13-14 even in
   good focus — meaning the camera was permanently misclassified as
   blurred. The first diagnosis attempt (adding sustained-window smoothing)
   was a real improvement but not the actual fix. The real root cause was
   found via temporary diagnostic logging: earlier manual testing had been
   measuring the ANNOTATED frame (which has synthetic sharp overlay
   graphics drawn on it) instead of the RAW camera frame, making the
   camera look sharper than it actually was. Once measured correctly, the
   threshold was recalibrated to 8.0 with clear margin, and documented as
   camera-dependent rather than universal.

3. A COVERED CAMERA STOPPED RESPONDING PERMANENTLY
   Some camera/driver combinations, under sustained near-total darkness
   (a covered lens), stop returning valid frames from the OS capture layer
   entirely (not just dark frames — actual read failures). The original
   reader loop kept calling .read() on the same broken capture handle
   forever, with no recovery path even after the lens was uncovered. Fix:
   detect sustained read failure and release + reopen the capture handle
   on a 5-second backoff until it recovers.

4. DEDUP_HASH UNIQUE CONSTRAINT CRASHED THE CORRELATOR
   The Incident.dedup_hash column originally had a database-level UNIQUE
   constraint. The correlator's own logic already correctly scoped
   "duplicate" to OPEN incidents only — meaning the same hash legitimately
   recurring after an earlier incident closed was expected, correct
   behavior, which the blanket UNIQUE constraint didn't allow, causing a
   crash on that exact common case. Fixed via an Alembic batch-mode
   migration removing the DB-level constraint (SQLite requires a full
   table rebuild to drop a constraint, since it can't ALTER one in place).

5. A DATABASE CONCURRENCY BUG SURFACED ONLY UNDER HIGHER WRITE LOAD
   SQLAlchemy's StaticPool (which forces a single shared connection across
   all threads) was being applied unconditionally, including to the real
   file-based production database — appropriate ONLY for `:memory:` SQLite,
   where it's required. Under light write activity this went unnoticed;
   once fire/smoke and line-crossing detection were added (increasing
   write frequency from the detection pipeline's background thread
   overlapping with HTTP request threads), it started producing "cannot
   commit — no transaction is active" race errors. Fixed by restricting
   StaticPool to in-memory URLs only.

6. A COVERED CAMERA LOOKED LIKE "OFFLINE" IN THE UI EVEN THOUGH THE
   BACKEND CORRECTLY SAID "BLACKOUT"
   Verified via live testing (polling the live camera-status endpoint
   continuously while physically covering the lens) that the backend
   correctly and promptly classified the state as "blackout," never
   "offline." The actual bug was in the frontend: the camera tile's
   `showVideo` condition included active/frozen/blurred but NOT blackout,
   so the tile fell back to the same generic "No signal" / WifiOff
   placeholder used for a truly offline camera — visually indistinguishable
   even though the badge text was technically correct. Fixed by including
   blackout in the video-visible states, so a covered camera now shows its
   actual (near-black) live feed instead of a generic offline placeholder.

7. THE LIVE FEED WAS RUNNING AT ~4-6 FPS AND NOBODY HAD MEASURED WHY
   Diagnosed with direct per-stage benchmarking rather than guessing:
   measured on a real frame with 3 people detected, YOLO cost ~125ms and a
   full MediaPipe pose-estimation model (run once per detected person, per
   frame, purely to draw a "Standing"/"Analyzing..." text label) cost
   another ~88ms — nearly 40% of total per-frame time — for output that
   was verified, by exhaustive codebase search, to be used NOWHERE else
   (not in fall detection, which is a separate real geometric heuristic;
   not in scoring; not stored anywhere). Removed entirely, along with its
   now-unused mediapipe dependency. Measured result after the fix: live
   MJPEG delivery went from an estimated ~4-6fps ceiling to a measured
   12.5fps, close to the stream's own 15fps cap.

8. DEAD CODE FROM THE ORIGINAL PORT
   Two modules (`enhanced_detector.py`, `person_tracker.py`) survived from
   the original reference codebase this was built on top of but were never
   actually imported anywhere in the live pipeline — confirmed by an
   exhaustive reference search. Left as a known, disclosed housekeeping
   item rather than silently ignored (see Limitations) — the honest answer
   to "why is this file here" is "leftover from the initial port, verified
   unused, safe to delete, just hasn't been."

12. KNOWN LIMITATIONS & HONEST SHORTCOMINGS
================================================================================

DETECTION ACCURACY
- Fall, abandoned-object, and fire/smoke detection are classical CV
  heuristics, not trained/validated classifiers. Their confidence is
  deliberately capped low in the scoring system for exactly this reason —
  expect real false positives and false negatives, by design, not by
  oversight. Every one of them requires human verification before any
  action is taken.
- Fall detection needs a mostly front-on, full-body camera view; a close
  or steep webcam-style angle will never trigger it (verified directly).
- Abandoned-object detection needs ~300 frames for its background model to
  converge, and fails to fire if a person remains near the object
  (overlap suppression).
- No low-light/IR tuning has been done; performance in poor lighting is
  untested and likely degraded, especially for the color-based fire/smoke
  heuristic.
- No testing under adverse outdoor conditions (fog, rain, glare).
- Simple centroid tracking can be confused by heavy occlusion (multiple
  people fully overlapping) — no Kalman filtering or appearance-based
  re-association, by design (see Section 5), but that is a real accuracy
  tradeoff in dense-crowd scenes specifically.
- No evaluation has been done for demographic/appearance bias in the
  underlying YOLO person-detection model — this is a documented,
  industry-wide open issue for object detection models generally, and this
  project has not independently audited it.

PERFORMANCE / SCALE
- CPU-bound YOLO inference sets a real per-camera frame-rate ceiling
  (~12-15fps measured on typical hardware after fixing the MediaPipe
  bottleneck). One Python thread per camera does not scale cleanly to
  dozens/hundreds of simultaneous cameras on one machine — that needs GPU
  batching or a multi-node/edge architecture that isn't built yet.
- SQLite is appropriate for a single-node deployment, not proven at
  high-concurrency multi-node scale. The Postgres migration path exists via
  SQLAlchemy but has not been load-tested.
- No formal load/stress testing against many simultaneous camera streams.

SECURITY / COMPLIANCE
- No formal third-party penetration test or security audit has been
  performed.
- JWT-only auth — no SSO/LDAP/Active Directory integration yet for
  enterprise deployment.
- Evidence clips live on local disk, not redundant/replicated storage —
  the cryptographic signing proves IF something was altered, but doesn't
  protect against outright loss of the underlying storage (no backup
  strategy built in yet).
- No formal DPDP Act (India) or GDPR compliance certification. The
  architecture is designed around the PRINCIPLES those laws care about
  (no biometrics, redaction by default, full audit trail) but that is not
  the same as a legal compliance review, which has not been done.
- No adversarial-robustness evaluation of the YOLO model (e.g., resistance
  to printed adversarial patterns) has been performed.

PRODUCT SCOPE
- No mobile app — web dashboard only.
- Notifications currently go to Telegram only, not SMS/email/WhatsApp.
- Crowd threshold and line-crossing are global/single-line, not yet
  zone-aware with time-of-day policy layering.
- No historical trend analytics or false-positive-rate tracking to help
  operators tune thresholds over time — thresholds are static and
  admin-configured only.
- The access-control-system integration hook that scoring.py already
  anticipates (`access_event_matched`) has no real subsystem behind it yet
  — it's a deliberately-kept parameter, not a hidden gap, but it means
  there's currently no way to automatically reduce a restricted-zone-entry
  alert's severity based on a valid badge swipe, for example.

HOUSEKEEPING
- Two backend modules from the original ported codebase
  (`enhanced_detector.py`, `person_tracker.py`) are dead code — verified
  unused anywhere in the live pipeline, not yet deleted. Disclosed
  honestly rather than hidden; harmless but should eventually be removed.

================================================================================
14. COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE & DIFFERENTIATION
================================================================================

Most existing "AI CCTV analytics" products fall into one of two camps:
(a) they lead with facial recognition / biometric identity as the
headline feature, carrying real privacy and legal exposure, or (b) they
provide an opaque AI confidence score with no explanation of how it was
reached, which is close to useless as defensible evidence in an actual
incident review or legal context.

EyeSpy's differentiation is the combination of three things most
competitors don't do together:
1. Every alert has a deterministic, human-readable explanation traceable
   to one specific rule — not a black-box score.
2. Confidence and impact are reported and capped SEPARATELY, so an
   unreliable heuristic can never be mistaken for a certain detection.
3. Evidence is cryptographically dual-signed and every operator action is
   hash-chain audit-logged, so the system's own output and history can
   both be independently verified, not just trusted on faith.

None of this requires proprietary camera hardware — it works with
whatever RTSP/USB cameras a site already owns, which lowers the switching
cost versus a vendor-locked VMS platform considerably.

17. EXTENSIVE Q&A BANK
================================================================================

--- ON THE CORE PHILOSOPHY ---

Q: Why not use facial recognition — wouldn't that make it more useful?
A: Deliberately and permanently excluded. It creates real privacy,
consent, and legal exposure (increasingly restricted globally, including
under India's DPDP Act), and most real security needs don't actually
require knowing WHO someone is — they require knowing THAT someone is
somewhere they shouldn't be, or doing something unsafe. Anonymous,
camera-local tracking achieves the actual security goal without that
liability.

Q: Isn't "no facial recognition" a limitation, not a feature? Competitors
can identify repeat offenders.
A: It's a deliberate boundary, not a missing feature we ran out of time
for. Identity tracking is exactly the part of "AI surveillance" facing the
most legal and public pushback right now — building around behavior
instead of identity is what makes this deployable somewhere a
facial-recognition system would get rejected or banned outright, which is
a real commercial and ethical advantage, not a gap.

Q: What if the system misses an actual fall or fire?
A: Any single detector, human or automated, can miss something — that's
true of any monitoring system. What EyeSpy adds on top of passive human
monitoring is a system watching continuously without attention fatigue,
that never gets tired or distracted, running heuristics tuned to reduce
that risk. It's explicitly not sold or positioned as a guaranteed replacement
for human judgment — it's a decision-support layer that surfaces things a
distracted human might miss, while always keeping a human in the loop for
the final call.

--- ON ACCURACY / RELIABILITY ---

Q: How accurate is the system overall?
A: Person detection itself (YOLOv8n) is a well-validated, heavily
benchmarked model. The behavior heuristics on top of it (fall, abandoned
object, fire/smoke) are classical computer vision, not trained
classifiers, and the system says so explicitly to its own operators —
their confidence scores are hard-capped low, and every alert from them
requires human verification. We'd rather state that boundary clearly than
claim an accuracy number we can't back up with a proper validation study.

Q: What's your false-positive rate?
A: We don't have a formal measured false-positive rate from a large
labeled dataset — that's honestly a gap, and it's why every heuristic
output is explicitly capped at low confidence and routed to a human,
rather than presented as something to trust and act on automatically.
Building that measurement (via the false_positive/true_positive
disposition operators already record on resolution) into a tuning
feedback loop is on the roadmap.

Q: How do you handle multiple people or crowds?
A: The centroid tracker handles moderate density well; heavy occlusion
(people fully overlapping) can confuse a simple centroid-based tracker,
which is a known tradeoff of choosing that approach over a heavier
Kalman-filter/appearance-based tracker (see the tracking design rationale).
Headcount for crowd detection itself is reliable, since it's just counting
already-detected boxes, not a fuzzy inference.

Q: Can it work at night or in low light?
A: Not specifically tuned for it yet — this is an honest, disclosed
limitation. IR-camera support and low-light tuning are explicit future
work, not something claimed to work today.

Q: What about outdoor cameras in fog, rain, or glare?
A: Untested. This would likely degrade the color-based fire/smoke
heuristic and the blackout/blur camera-health checks in particular, and is
disclosed as future testing work rather than a validated capability today.

--- ON PRIVACY / ETHICS / COMPLIANCE ---

Q: Is this GDPR/DPDP Act compliant?
A: It's architected around the principles those laws care about — no
biometric collection, redaction by default, a full audit trail of who
accessed what — but it has not gone through a formal legal compliance
review or certification. That's future work, not a compliance claim being
made today.

Q: What stops an operator from misusing the system — viewing footage they
shouldn't?
A: Role-based access control enforced at the API layer (not just hidden in
the UI), and every access to unredacted evidence is written into the
hash-chained audit log — if someone tried to edit that log afterward to
hide what they did, the hash chain breaks and it's independently
detectable via the verify endpoint.

Q: Has the system been security-audited or penetration-tested?
A: No, and we say so directly — that's a real, disclosed gap for anyone
considering a production or sensitive deployment, and formal third-party
security review is explicit future work before any sensitive rollout.

Q: How long is evidence retained, and can someone request deletion (right
to erasure)?
A: There's no automatic retention/deletion policy built in yet — evidence
persists on local disk until manually removed. This is a real gap worth
having an honest answer for: a formal retention policy, and reconciling a
"right to erasure" request against the tamper-evident AUDIT LOG (which by
design should NOT be alterable) versus the underlying EVIDENCE CLIPS
(which reasonably could be deleted on a schedule or on request) is
explicit future work, not solved today.

Q: Could the YOLO model have demographic bias in who it detects?
A: This is a well-documented, industry-wide open question for object
detection models generally, and this project has not independently
audited it for this specific model. Worth being upfront about rather than
asserting fairness without having actually measured it.

--- ON ARCHITECTURE / TECHNICAL CHOICES ---

Q: Why Python/FastAPI instead of a lower-level language for the real-time
video processing?
A: The real-time-critical part (YOLO inference) already runs through
PyTorch's compiled/optimized backend regardless of the surrounding
language — the orchestration layer (reading frames, running heuristics,
writing to a database, serving an API) isn't the bottleneck, so Python's
development speed and library ecosystem (OpenCV, Ultralytics, FastAPI, all
first-class in Python) was the right tradeoff, not a performance
compromise in the part that actually matters for speed.

Q: Why threads per camera instead of async or multiprocessing?
A: Each camera's reader loop and detection loop are genuinely blocking,
CPU/IO-heavy work (cv2 capture reads, YOLO inference) — async coroutines
don't help with CPU-bound work without offloading to a thread/process pool
anyway, and Python threads were sufficient at the target scale (a handful
of cameras) without the added complexity of inter-process communication
that a multiprocessing approach would need for shared state like the
tracker and health monitor. Multiprocessing/GPU batching is the right
answer at a much larger camera count — see Future Roadmap.

Q: Why SQLite instead of Postgres/MySQL from the start?
A: Zero setup for the target deployment size (one building, one small
server, a handful to a few dozen cameras) — no separate database server
to install, run, and secure. Because the whole data layer goes through
SQLAlchemy, moving to Postgres for a larger deployment is a connection-
string change, not a rewrite; that migration path exists by construction
even though it hasn't been load-tested at scale yet.

Q: Why not use a proper multi-object tracker like DeepSORT?
A: DeepSORT-class trackers solve a harder problem than this system
actually has — surviving long occlusion and re-identifying a track after
it's lost, which requires an appearance-embedding model on top of
detection. This system deliberately does NOT want tracks to survive
loss-and-reacquisition, because that starts to resemble identity tracking,
which conflicts with the "anonymous, camera-local-only" design principle.
A simple nearest-centroid tracker does the actual needed job — count and
distinguish currently-visible people — with far less compute and a track
ID that's trivially provable as meaningless outside the current moment.

Q: Why classical CV heuristics instead of training your own fall/fire/
abandoned-object detection models?
A: Training a reliable model for any of these needs a large, well-labeled,
situation-realistic dataset that doesn't exist off-the-shelf in sufficient
quantity or quality, and collecting/labeling one is a project of its own.
Classical CV requires zero training data, is fully deterministic, and —
critically, since every one of these three is explicitly "flag for human
review," never "trust automatically" — its failure modes are
understandable by a human ("the bounding box went wide and flat"), unlike
a neural network's internal decision. The honestly-worse raw accuracy
versus an eventual trained model is exactly why their confidence is capped
low in scoring, rather than presented as equally trustworthy to person
detection itself.

Q: Why MJPEG instead of WebRTC for the live video feed?
A: WebRTC solves sub-second two-way call latency via signaling/ICE/SDP
negotiation — complexity this project doesn't need for one-way, dashboard-
side monitoring, where an extra 100-300ms of latency is a non-issue. MJPEG
is a single long-lived HTTP response of JPEG frames — the entire client
implementation is one `<img>` tag, with the browser handling reconnection
natively on drop.

Q: Why both a WebSocket AND polling for live dashboard updates, isn't that
redundant?
A: They cover each other's failure mode. A dropped or missed WebSocket
message means the UI would silently go stale forever with no self-
correction if that were the only mechanism. The 8-second poll guarantees
the dashboard is never more than 8 seconds stale even if a push event is
missed, while the WebSocket gives near-instant updates the rest of the
time — a small redundancy cost for a real reliability guarantee.

Q: Why Ed25519 in addition to HMAC — why not just pick one signature
scheme?
A: They prove different things to different audiences. HMAC (a shared
secret) proves internal authenticity to anyone holding that secret — us.
Ed25519 (public-key) proves the same authenticity to a third party — a
court, an external auditor — who only has the PUBLIC key and never had
access to the shared secret used internally. Relying on HMAC alone would
mean handing an external verifier the same secret used to sign everything
else, which would also let them forge new "signed" evidence.

Q: Why a hash-chained log instead of a blockchain for the audit trail?
A: Nothing here needs decentralized consensus or multiple untrusted
parties agreeing on order — it's a single organization's chain of custody.
A straightforward hash chain (each row's hash includes the previous row's
hash) gives the same tamper-evidence property — any edit to a past row
breaks every hash after it — without the operational cost, complexity, and
overhead a blockchain would add for zero corresponding benefit at this
scale.

Q: Why shadcn/ui instead of a standard component library like Material UI?
A: shadcn generates actual component source directly into the project
rather than being installed as an opaque dependency, built on unstyled,
accessibility-solved Radix primitives underneath. That gave full control
to restyle and extend components (e.g. adding new Badge variants for
camera-health states) without fighting a library's theming API or waiting
on an upstream release for a small change.

--- ON BUSINESS / DIFFERENTIATION ---

Q: How is this different from existing CCTV/VMS analytics products?
A: Most competitors either lead with facial recognition (privacy/legal
exposure) or provide an opaque AI score with no explanation (useless as
defensible evidence). EyeSpy's combination of deterministic, rule-
traceable explanations, separated confidence/impact scoring, and
cryptographically-verifiable evidence and audit trails is not something
most existing products do together.

Q: What's the cost of deploying this?
A: Designed to work with a site's EXISTING CCTV/RTSP cameras and
commodity server hardware — no proprietary camera hardware required — and
the entire stack is open-source technology (Python/FastAPI/React/YOLO),
which keeps licensing cost far lower than a vendor-locked VMS platform.

Q: How do you scale to hundreds of cameras?
A: Honestly, not yet — today it's one CPU-bound processing thread per
camera, which is fine for a building or small campus but would need GPU
batching or an edge-node architecture to scale to a large multi-site
deployment. That's explicit, disclosed future work, not solved today.

Q: Who's the competition, and why would a customer switch?
A: Existing CCTV/VMS analytics vendors, both international and Indian
security-tech players, generally either add facial recognition (privacy/
legal liability) or offer opaque AI scoring with no evidence-integrity
guarantee. The pitch to a security-conscious buyer — a campus, hospital,
or government facility — is: the same detection value, none of the
biometric liability, and evidence that's actually defensible in a review
or legal context because it's cryptographically signed and audit-logged.

Q: What's the actual go-to-market plan?
A: Most realistically, through existing security-system integrators who
already have camera-install relationships with target customers (campuses,
hospitals, offices) — selling the software intelligence layer on top of
hardware they're already installing, rather than competing to replace
cameras outright.

Q: Is there IP here, or is this just gluing together open-source
components?
A: The individual building blocks (YOLO, FastAPI, React) are open source —
the actual value and engineering is in the system design around them: the
deterministic scoring/explanation layer that turns raw detections into
defensible, explainable incidents; the confidence/impact separation
principle applied consistently across every heuristic; and the dual-
signing + hash-chain evidence architecture. That system-level design, not
any single component, is what's differentiated.
